# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Practical Survey

## TL;DR
- Small language models (SLMs) are best understood not merely by parameter count but by whether their architecture, memory footprint, and runtime fit a target device; a useful surveyed range is roughly 100M–5B parameters. [1][2]
- Compression methods are complementary: distillation transfers behavior, quantization reduces numerical precision, pruning removes computation or weights, and low-rank/shared structures reduce redundancy. The deployment winner depends on hardware and workload, not compression ratio alone. [3][4]
- Inference has two different bottlenecks: prefill is generally compute-bound and determines first-token responsiveness, whereas decoding is often memory-bound and determines sustained token latency. [1][5]
- The strongest recent systems combine model compression with cache management and speculative execution: reported results include up to 2.5× speedup for QuantSpec, 3.4× for TinyServe, and 65% lower generation memory for SpecMemo in their respective settings. [6][7][8]
- Quality, latency, memory, energy, cost, and safety must be measured together under a specified workload and hardware; FLOPs or tokens/second alone are unreliable proxies for user experience. [9][10][11]

## Background

Efficient inference is the problem of producing useful model outputs within constraints on latency, memory, energy, cost, privacy, and reliability. SLMs are a central response: the SLM survey scopes them as decoder-only Transformers with approximately 100M–5B parameters, intended for settings ranging from low-end IoT devices and wearables to phones and tablets. [1] This is a deployment definition as much as a size definition. A model that is “small” on a data-center GPU may still be impractical on a phone once weights, runtime buffers, tokenizer state, and the key-value (KV) cache are included.

The case for local inference has strengthened because cloud calls introduce network latency, outages, recurring cost, and privacy exposure. Edge-first analysis adds reliability and offline operation to the motivation, while recognizing that cloud fallback remains useful when local capacity or scale is insufficient. [2] Efficient inference therefore concerns a system pipeline, not only a neural network: model architecture, numerical format, compiler and kernels, scheduling, cache policy, and hardware jointly determine performance.

A useful mental model separates prefilling from decoding. Prefill processes the prompt in parallel and creates the KV cache; decoding then generates tokens sequentially. The former tends to be compute-bound, while the latter is often memory-bound, and the two stages can respond differently to the same optimization. [1][5] Accordingly, first-token latency (TTFT), time per generated token, throughput, peak memory, and energy per request should be reported separately. Foundational distillation work made this deployment perspective concrete. DistilBERT framed a smaller student as a way to retain useful behavior from a larger teacher under constrained inference budgets. [12]

## Compression and architecture: reducing the model before serving

**Distillation transfers capability rather than simply deleting parameters.** TinyBERT distills embeddings, hidden states, attention matrices, and logits, using general distillation during pretraining followed by task-specific distillation after fine-tuning. It achieves comparable GLUE results; the paper also reports 7.5× smaller size and 9.4× faster inference than BERTBASE in its comparison. [13] MobileBERT takes a related but architecturally deliberate route: bottlenecks and an inverted-bottleneck BERTLARGE teacher yield a model reported as 4.3× smaller and 5.5× faster than BERTBASE, with 62 ms latency on a Pixel 4. [14]

Distillation’s strength is that it can preserve task behavior unavailable from naïve parameter removal. Its weakness is the cost and complexity of producing the teacher targets: white-box methods need teacher parameters or logits, while black-box methods can use outputs but may lose richer internal supervision. [4] Distillation also tends to inherit teacher biases and failure modes unless the data and objectives are deliberately changed. Thus, it is most valuable when a high-quality teacher and representative student training data are available.

**Quantization changes the arithmetic representation.** Post-training quantization (PTQ) can be applied after training, while quantization-aware training (QAT) retrains with quantization effects in the loop. The main choices are weight-only, weight-activation, and KV-cache quantization. [3] Weight-only methods reduce storage but may require dequantization before multiplication; KV-cache quantization instead targets the growing memory cost of long contexts. Activation-aware methods protect sensitive weights: the SLM survey describes AWQ as protecting 1% of critical weights and reports 3–4× speedup over FP16 with 4× lower memory in its cited setting. [1]

The important qualification is that a smaller representation does not guarantee proportional end-to-end acceleration. Kernels must support the format, and quantization can help prefill more than decode—or vice versa—depending on context length and hardware. In Phi-1.5 measurements, one edge study reports at least 25% lower short-prompt prefill latency after quantization, but only a 13% reduction for its best Q4_K_M result at a 50-token prompt. [5]

**Pruning, sharing, and factorization remove redundancy, but deployability matters.** Unstructured pruning removes individual weights and can achieve high nominal sparsity, but its irregular memory access often fails to produce speedups on general hardware. Semi-structured and structured pruning remove blocks, neurons, heads, or layers and are more compatible with optimized kernels. A post-training pruning framework explicitly targets structured sparsity under a resource constraint without requiring retraining. [3][4] Low-rank factorization replaces a matrix with two smaller matrices, while parameter sharing reuses weights across layers. Both reduce storage; sharing, however, need not reduce hidden size or the number of sequential Transformer layers, so memory savings do not automatically become lower latency. [13]

Efficient architectures make these trade-offs during design rather than after training. MobileBERT reduces width rather than mainly reducing depth, and the edge deployment study finds that architecture can affect latency more than model size below 1B parameters. Vocabulary size also matters disproportionately because it increases memory and compute-buffer requirements. [5][14] The broad lesson is that “parameter count” is an incomplete optimization target: bottlenecks, factorization, vocabulary, attention implementation, and hardware-friendly structure can dominate real runtime.

## Runtime systems: cache, speculation, and hardware-aware serving

Once a model is compressed, serving optimizations attack repeated work and memory movement. Multi-turn conversations are a clear case: conventional engines may recompute historical KV caches, while CachedAttention proposes hierarchical KV-cache reuse across turns. [15] This is especially valuable when prompts contain a stable system instruction or conversation history, though cache reuse requires careful invalidation and memory policies.

Long-context inference makes the KV cache a first-class resource. QuantSpec combines self-speculative decoding with a hierarchical 4-bit KV cache and 4-bit weights. It reports acceptance rates above 90%, up to approximately 2.5× end-to-end speedup, and about 1.3× lower memory than the compared self-speculative alternatives. [6] These results illustrate why cache compression and speculative execution are complementary: quantization increases the feasible cache, while accepted draft tokens reduce sequential target-model work.

Query-aware sparsity is another route. TinyServe targets 125M–350M models such as TinyLLaMA and GPT2-345M, selecting relevant KV pages using page metadata and fusing scoring, sparse memory access, and masked attention in a CUDA kernel. Its experiments report up to 3.4× speedup and more than 2× memory savings with negligible accuracy drop, while integrating with vLLM’s PagedAttention kernel. [7] The benefit depends on finding irrelevant cache pages without harming attention quality; it is therefore a workload- and query-dependent optimization rather than a universal replacement for dense attention.

Speculative decoding can also be managed as a device resource. SpecMemo dynamically controls speculative-decoding memory and reports retaining 96% of speculative throughput while reducing generation memory by 65% on a single Titan RTX in its MT-Bench setting. For batched, distributed Llama-2-70B-Chat inference on eight MI250 GPUs, it reports 2× speedup over vanilla decoding and up to 8× throughput at batch size 10. [8] Although the latter is not an SLM result, it demonstrates a general point: draft length, parallel heads, cache allocation, batch size, and acceptance rate must be co-optimized. TinyLLM similarly frames edge deployment as a joint training-and-deployment problem, motivated by the latency, connectivity, and privacy costs of remote inference. [16]

## Evaluation, limitations, and safety

A credible comparison needs a vector of measurements. The Efficiency Pentathlon evaluates throughput, latency, memory overhead, parameter count, and energy on controlled hardware, arguing that FLOPs often fail to predict real throughput, latency, or energy because implementations and operations differ. It also recommends total-machine energy, not GPU-only energy, because CPU, DRAM, I/O, utilization, and idle time contribute materially. [9] The right objective is application-specific: an interactive assistant may prioritize TTFT and tail latency, whereas offline processing may prioritize throughput and energy.

Energy measurement is itself a research problem. ML.ENERGY constructs time–energy Pareto frontiers and selects configurations satisfying a latency target. In one chatbot example, a configuration with 77 ms average output-token time reduced energy per generation by 44% relative to the latency-minimizing configuration while meeting a 100 ms target. [10] Per-token energy is not sufficient when systems produce different output lengths; benchmarks should report the task, output distribution, measurement boundary, warm-up policy, cache state, batch and arrival pattern, quantization, runtime, hardware topology, failures, and behavior near saturation. [10]

Quality evaluation must also be broader than accuracy. HELM covers accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency across 16 scenarios and seven metric categories. Before standardization, evaluated models averaged only 17.9% coverage of its core scenarios, versus 96.0% for 30 models under the framework, showing how incomplete coverage can make comparisons misleading. [11] Small models may also have qualitatively different safety behavior. A study of 63 SLMs from 15 families, spanning 100M–7B parameters, found 47.6% with attack-success rates above 40% and 38.1% above 50% for direct harmful queries in its evaluation; no tested defense worked perfectly across models and attacks. [17] Parameter size alone was not a sufficient safety proxy, so compression claims must not be presented as safety claims.

## Trends and open problems

Recent work is converging on deployment-aware co-design: low-bit weights and caches, structured sparsity, speculative execution, and query-aware scheduling are being combined rather than evaluated in isolation. [2][6][7] The next challenge is reproducible, cross-device evidence. Reported speedups can change with prompt length, cache reuse, kernels, batch size, and whether measurements include tokenization, queuing, or data transfer. A model that wins TTFT may lose energy or tail latency, and a model that fits in memory may still be too slow for interactive use.

Three questions remain especially open. First, deployment-aware scaling laws should jointly model quality, parameter count, token data, device memory, and compute; the SLM survey argues that conventional training-only scaling misses these constraints. [1] Second, benchmark design needs workload distributions and goodput, not only arithmetic throughput: saturated systems can produce more tokens while violating latency objectives. [10] Third, robustness and privacy must be evaluated on-device with the same rigor as capability. Small models may be easier to deploy privately, but their jailbreak resistance, memorization, and behavior under distribution shift are not implied by their size. [11][17]

The practical conclusion is therefore conditional: choose the smallest model that meets the task-quality and trust requirements, then optimize its entire execution path. Distillation and architecture establish the capacity–quality point; quantization and structured sparsity make it fit; cache and speculative methods determine long-context and decode behavior; and controlled multi-metric evaluation determines whether the apparent efficiency is real.
