# Video and Multimodal Generation: Architectures, Progress, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- Video generation has moved from discrete latent next-token prediction toward latent diffusion Transformers that treat space-time patches as scalable tokens, improving fidelity and variable-resolution training while retaining major compute costs [1][2][3].
- The field is converging on multimodal systems: one model or coordinated stack can accept text, images, video, and audio, but unified interfaces still commonly rely on specialized encoders and decoders rather than one shared generator [4][5][6].
- Recent progress is strongest in controllability, duration, resolution, and audiovisual conditioning: Open-Sora reports a 79.76% total VBench score at 240p/2 seconds, while newer systems target minute-long or natively audio-video outputs [7][8][9].
- The central scientific gap is not merely visual sharpness: TC-Bench finds most models realize less than 20% of requested compositional changes, and evaluation still misses physics, camera motion, safety, and temporal meaning [10][11][12].

## Background
Video generation models learn a distribution over visual appearance and motion, with the additional requirement that frames remain coherent over time. Multimodal generation broadens the problem to conditioning or producing multiple signal types—especially text, images, video, and audio. This matters now because a single representation can potentially support editing, simulation, creative production, and interactive agents, while larger datasets and compute make high-resolution generation practical enough to expose deeper semantic and physical failures [3].

A foundational design is VideoGPT’s two-stage pipeline: a 3D-convolutional VQ-VAE with axial self-attention compresses video into discrete latents, and a GPT-like autoregressive Transformer models those latents with spatio-temporal positional encodings [1]. This reframes video synthesis as next-token prediction, but sequence length and tokenizer quality become bottlenecks. Diffusion changed the efficiency-quality balance. MagicVideo performs denoising in a low-dimensional latent space using a VAE, 3D U-Net, frame-wise adaptor, and directed temporal attention; at 256×256 it reports roughly 64× fewer FLOPs than the cited pixel-space baseline [13].

The current scaling paradigm combines these ideas with Transformer tokenization. Sora compresses images and videos, turns the latent representation into space-time patches, and trains a diffusion Transformer over variable durations, resolutions, and aspect ratios; images can be represented as one-frame videos [3]. Its public report says the largest model can produce up to one minute of video, but also documents failures in basic physical interactions, long-duration coherence, and object permanence [3]. Thus, the field’s key question is shifting from “can a model produce plausible frames?” to “can it maintain a controllable, causally coherent multimodal world over time?”

## From discrete autoregression to latent diffusion Transformers
Discrete autoregressive models have a clear conceptual advantage: they reduce generation to a familiar likelihood objective and can use one decoder-only Transformer for heterogeneous token streams. VideoGPT reports competitive samples on BAIR Robot and high-fidelity results on UCF-101 and TGIF [1]. VideoPoet extends this strategy to images, video, text, and audio, combining multimodal pretraining with task adaptation. In its reported zero-shot text-to-video setting, it obtains MSR-VTT CLIPSIM 0.3049 and FVD 213, UCF-101 FVD 355 and IS 38.44; adaptation raises MSR-VTT CLIPSIM to 0.3123 [5]. Such a model naturally supports frame prediction, inpainting, and outpainting, but sequential token generation is costly and quality depends on a long discrete sequence.

Latent diffusion instead iteratively denoises a continuous compressed representation. The review literature classifies more than two hundred diffusion video methods and identifies strong controllability, competitive visual quality, and compatibility with multimodal inputs as recurring advantages [14]. Latte illustrates the modern diffusion-Transformer direction: a VAE produces spatio-temporal tokens, while factorized Transformer variants separate or decompose spatial and temporal processing to reduce the attention burden; it reports state-of-the-art results on FaceForensics, SkyTimelapse, UCF101, and Taichi-HD [2]. Diffusion is more parallel than autoregressive decoding and handles continuous control well, but many denoising steps, long sequences, and memory-intensive attention remain expensive.

The practical compromise is factorization and compression. Open-Sora’s STDiT decouples spatial attention within frames from temporal attention across corresponding spatial locations, while a compressive 3D autoencoder reduces the token count. Its reported 1.2 model grows from a 580M image DiT initialization to 1.1B parameters and supports text-to-image, text-to-video, and image-to-video outputs up to 15 seconds and 720p [7]. On 240p, two-second VBench, it reports 79.76% total, 81.35% quality, and 73.39% semantic scores, above the cited CogVideo and Latte totals [7]. These figures are useful within that protocol, but they do not establish long-horizon physical correctness.

## Unified multimodal generation and controllability
There are two meanings of “unified.” A shared token model, exemplified by VideoPoet, directly processes multimodal sequences and can transfer an autoregressive interface across tasks [4][5]. A modular any-to-any system, exemplified by NExT-GPT, uses an LLM as the reasoning hub, modality-specific encoders and adaptors for understanding, and diffusion decoders for output. NExT-GPT tunes only selected projection layers—about 1% of parameters—and reports text-to-image FID 10.07, audio FAD 1.68, video CLIPSIM 31.97, plus MSVD-QA 64.5 and MSRVTT-QA 61.4 [6]. Modularity lowers training cost and makes it easier to add modalities, but the result depends on multiple pretrained components and interface alignment rather than a single shared pixel-level generator.

Conditioning is expanding beyond a text prompt. UniVG combines text and image conditions through multi-condition cross-attention and biased Gaussian noise for unified-modal video tasks [15]. Commercial and research systems increasingly support image-to-video, reference images, key-framing, camera controls, and motion controls. Runway’s Gen-3 Alpha is described as jointly trained on images and video and designed for Motion Brush, advanced camera controls, and Director Mode; its temporally dense captions are intended to support precise transitions and key-framing [16]. These mechanisms address a weakness of unconstrained text-to-video: prompt adherence alone does not specify the identity, trajectory, composition, or camera path needed for editing.

Audio is an especially revealing multimodal test because synchronization is temporal rather than merely semantic. MMAudio uses video, optional text, frame-level conditioning, flow matching, and larger text-audio data to synthesize synchronized audio from video [9]. Newer systems claim native dialogue, ambience, and sound effects alongside video, while also supporting reference images and higher-resolution outputs [17]. The benefit is a more complete audiovisual artifact; the unresolved problem is whether speech, actions, causal sound, and scene timing remain consistently aligned across longer clips.

## Scaling duration, resolution, and efficiency
Video cost grows rapidly with pixels, frames, and denoising steps. Imagen Video demonstrates a cascade: a base model generates 16 frames at 40×24 and 3 fps, followed by temporal and spatial super-resolution to reach 128 frames at 1280×768 and 24 fps—about 5.3 seconds [18]. Cascades make high-resolution synthesis tractable, but errors in the base sequence can be amplified by later stages. Sora’s variable-size patch representation makes mixed image/video training and flexible aspect ratios possible, yet its report still notes spontaneous object appearances and long-duration incoherence [3].

Recent work targets the quadratic attention bottleneck directly. LinGen argues that Diffusion Transformer cost scales quadratically with the number of pixels, helping explain why many systems remain around 10–20 seconds; it proposes linear-complexity processing and claims high-resolution, minute-length generation on one GPU without quality compromise [8]. This is an efficiency direction rather than a settled solution: longer output increases not only compute but also the probability of identity drift, event-order errors, and physically inconsistent state changes. Compression and linear attention therefore need to be evaluated jointly with long-horizon fidelity, not only throughput.

## Evaluation, safety, and unresolved failure modes
Standard metrics are incomplete. A survey of AI-generated video evaluation separates technical, dynamic, physical, consistency, quality, and alignment errors; VBench uses 16 hierarchical dimensions, while EvalCrafter combines visual quality, text-video alignment, motion quality, and temporal consistency [12]. FID and FVD compare feature distributions, and CLIP-based scores measure semantic relation, but neither necessarily detects a disappearing object, an impossible collision, or a wrong event order in an individual clip [19][12].

Temporal compositionality is a particularly sharp test. TC-Bench specifies initial and final scene states and measures whether requested components and relations change over time. Its authors report that most evaluated generators realize less than 20% of requested compositional changes, while the proposed completeness metrics correlate better with human judgments than existing metrics [10]. DynamicEval further argues that static-camera, subject-centric tests underrepresent camera motion and occlusion. Its 45,000 human annotations over 3,000 videos show failure cases for VBench motion smoothness; object-aware metrics improve correlation with human preference by more than two percentage points at video and model levels [11].

World consistency is consequently becoming a distinct target. The proposed World Consistency Score combines object permanence, relation stability, causal compliance, and flicker penalties, motivated by the gap between sharp imagery and plausible physics [19]. This complements rather than replaces broad quality metrics. The open evaluation challenge is to combine automated, human, prompt-grounded, and physically grounded tests without collapsing important failure types into one leaderboard number.

Safety must also be temporal and multimodal. T2VSafetyBench evaluates four safety categories and 14 critical aspects across nine text-to-video models; its reduced benchmark has 689 prompts and treats a sequence as unsafe when its unsafety score exceeds 0.5 [20]. It emphasizes that harmless-looking frames can form harmful content through continuity, and covers rights, misinformation, privacy, and synthetic identities. SAFESORA contains 14,711 prompts, 57,333 videos, and 51,691 human preference pairs; its text-video moderation model reaches 82.94% agreement with human judgment on its test set [21]. These resources expose a tension between usability and safety rather than solving it.

## Trends and open problems
The last two years show a coherent trajectory: scalable patch representations, image/video joint training, factorized or linear attention, richer reference and camera control, and native audiovisual generation [3][7][16][8][9]. The strongest systems are no longer merely text-to-video demos; they are multimodal interfaces for editing, continuation, sound, and structured control. Yet the evidence also suggests that capability is uneven: duration and resolution improve faster than causal reasoning, temporal composition, and reliable audio synchronization.

Several questions remain open. First, can models learn persistent state and physical relations rather than interpolate plausible appearances? Sora’s documented physical failures and TC-Bench’s sub-20% compositional-change result make this empirical, not philosophical [3][10]. Second, which representation best balances shared multimodal reasoning with modality-specific fidelity: one token space, a modular LLM-plus-decoder stack, or hybrids? Third, can evaluation become robust to camera motion, occlusion, prompt context, and long horizons while remaining affordable? Finally, training-data provenance, licensing, memorization, representativeness, and safety need to be treated as design constraints. The Copyright Office notes that training can involve millions or billions of works and that large-scale collection, filtering, and deduplication obscure what data were used and by whom [22]. Progress will therefore be measured not just by prettier clips, but by controllability, truthful temporal behavior, transparent data practices, and evidence that multimodal systems remain safe under realistic use.
