# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Methods, Evidence, and Open Problems

## TL;DR
- RL turns a language model into a policy whose token sequence is a reasoning trajectory; the reward may assess only the final answer or each intermediate step [1][2].
- The strongest practical recipe is not one algorithm: supervised cold starts or self-training, verifiable rewards, sampling/search, and policy optimization are combined in different proportions [3][4][5].
- GRPO reduces PPO’s value-model burden by estimating a baseline from groups of sampled answers; on DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B it raised GSM8K from 82.9% to 88.2% and MATH from 46.8% to 51.7% [4].
- Recent RLVR systems show striking benchmark gains, but evaluation can confuse capability with more attempts, calibration changes, or narrower solution support [5][6][7].
- The central research problem is therefore two-sided: make rewards verifiable enough to optimize, while measuring whether optimization produces transferable reasoning rather than shortcuts [8][9].

## Background

LLM reasoning is usually represented as a sequence of generated tokens: a question is the state, a chain of thought is a trajectory, and the answer is its terminal outcome. RL changes the training signal from next-token imitation to preference for trajectories that receive higher reward. In RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR), a deterministic checker commonly gives a binary reward for answer correctness, while correctness of the hidden chain of thought is treated separately because it is difficult to verify programmatically [2]. This makes mathematics, coding, and structured question answering unusually attractive: an answer, unit test, or symbolic result can often be checked without a human judge.

The conceptual precursor is chain-of-thought bootstrapping. STaR generates rationales, keeps examples whose answers are correct, fine-tunes on those rationales, and repeats; for initially incorrect questions it supplies the answer as a temporary hint while generating a rationale [3]. On CommonsenseQA, the reported system improved over a few-shot baseline by 35.9% and over direct-answer fine-tuning by 12.5%, reaching 72.5% versus 73.0% for a fine-tuned model 30 times larger [3]. This is not policy-gradient RL, but it establishes the essential loop: generate candidate reasoning, select it by outcome, and train the model toward selected trajectories.

The supervision choice remains fundamental. Outcome supervision scores only the final result; process supervision labels intermediate steps. Process supervision can locate an error and more directly reward a human-endorsed derivation, whereas outcome supervision can accept a correct answer reached through invalid reasoning [1]. The PRM800K release contains about 800,000 step-level labels across 75,000 solutions to 12,000 problems, and its process-supervised model solved 78.2% of a representative MATH subset [1]. Thus RL for reasoning is best understood as a family of credit-assignment choices, not simply “PPO applied to text.”

## Reward design and credit assignment

The simplest reward is a final-answer checker. It is cheap, reproducible, and scalable, but it provides sparse credit: every token in a long derivation is associated with the same terminal signal. Process reward models (PRMs) provide denser feedback by predicting step correctness after each step; the PRM800K formulation trains these predictions with language-model likelihood and scores a solution by multiplying step-correctness probabilities [1]. In best-of-500 selection, process supervision outperformed outcome reward modeling and final-answer checking at the reported data-collection scales [1]. Its weakness is cost and subjectivity: step labels require annotation, and a PRM can itself become a proxy to game.

Verifier-based rewards reverse that tradeoff. A math checker or unit test is objective and inexpensive at scale, but it may certify only the endpoint. The RLVR framework explicitly distinguishes answer correctness from chain-of-thought correctness and proposes CoT-Pass@K, which counts a sample only when both are correct [2]. Under a stated logic-prior assumption—correct chains are more likely than incorrect chains to yield correct answers—its analysis says GRPO can increase correct-chain probability even with answer-only rewards [2]. That is a useful hypothesis, not a general guarantee: it depends on the relationship between the verifier and the intended reasoning process.

The distinction explains why outcome reward can be effective in mathematics yet unsafe as a universal reasoning objective. OpenAI describes o1 as using RL to refine chains of thought, recover from mistakes, decompose difficult steps, and try alternate approaches; it also reports observing reward hacking in safety evaluations [10]. A reward that is reliable for exact arithmetic may be inadequate for truthfulness, explanation quality, or safety. Hybrid systems therefore use outcome verifiers for scale and process models, format checks, or human feedback to constrain behavior.

## Policy optimization and offline alternatives

Classical RLHF supplies a useful baseline. InstructGPT first uses supervised demonstrations, then trains a reward model on output comparisons, and finally optimizes the policy with PPO; a per-token KL penalty to the supervised policy limits reward-model over-optimization [11]. The same architecture can train reasoning, but PPO needs a value model and can be expensive when responses are long. Its reward model also inherits annotation and specification errors.

DeepSeekMath’s GRPO is a targeted alternative. It samples multiple outputs for one question, uses their average score as a group-relative baseline, and removes the critic/value model; its objective retains clipped policy ratios and a KL penalty [4]. The paper links this design to the difficulty of estimating token-level values when reward is assigned at the last token. With approximately 144K GSM8K/MATH questions, 64 samples per question, maximum length 1,024, learning rate 1e-6, and KL coefficient 0.04, RL improved DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B on GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH as above [4]. GRPO’s advantage is memory efficiency and a natural within-question comparison; its cost is many sampled trajectories and sensitivity to reward quality and group composition.

Policy optimization is not mandatory. ReST separates an offline Grow loop, which samples and scores outputs, from an Improve loop, which fine-tunes on selected high-reward samples; thresholds can rise across iterations, yielding smaller and better datasets [12]. STaR is a related self-training pattern, while rejection sampling and distillation can turn search into supervised learning [3][4]. These approaches reuse data and are often simpler to debug than online RL, but they may under-explore low-probability solutions and inherit selection bias. In practice, the boundary between “RL” and search-plus-imitation is porous: both generate candidate trajectories, apply a reward, and shift probability mass toward selected outputs.

## Scaling reasoning beyond mathematics

The 2024–2025 wave demonstrates that verifiable-reward RL can move from laboratory arithmetic to broad reasoning portfolios. DeepSeek-R1-Zero reportedly raised AIME 2024 pass@1 from 15.6% to 71.0% during RL, with majority voting reaching 86.7%; DeepSeek-R1 reported 79.8% AIME pass@1, 97.3% on MATH-500, 2,029 Codeforces Elo, and 71.5% on GPQA Diamond [5]. R1-Zero used large-scale RL without an initial SFT stage, whereas R1 added cold-start data and multi-stage training to address readability and language mixing [5]. These results suggest that outcome rewards can elicit latent reasoning behaviors, but they do not establish that every intermediate step is valid.

Open-Reasoner-Zero tests how much of the recipe is algorithmic complexity. Its reported 32B comparison used vanilla PPO with GAE, rule-based rewards, and no KL regularization; the paper reports requiring one-tenth as many training steps as the compared DeepSeek-R1-Zero pipeline [13]. The result favors transparent baselines and scaling, although the study itself centers mainly on math and general reasoning. RL is also being pushed into software and science: SWE-RL applies a lightweight rule-based reward to open-source software evolution, while OpenRFT reports an average 11% improvement on eight SciKnowEval tasks using only 100 domain-specific samples per task [14][15]. These extensions are promising precisely because their rewards connect to executable artifacts or domain answers, but they also expose the difficulty of defining correctness in less closed-ended settings.

## Failure modes and evaluation

Benchmark gains can conceal distributional and measurement effects. A recent position paper identifies budget mismatch, attempt inflation, calibration drift, and contamination as confounds in RLVR evaluation. It recommends budget-matched saturation curves with variance, calibration and abstention tracking, judge-robustness tests, and contamination screening; it argues that some gains shrink when budgets and calibration are controlled [6]. This means pass@1, pass@k, response length, and abstention should be reported together rather than treating one score as reasoning ability.

A second concern is support collapse. The Invisible Leash study characterizes RLVR as amplifying high-reward outputs already present in the base model. Across reported experiments, it says RL often gains single-sample precision while losing more correct completions than it gains; for ProRL-1.5B, the shrinkage-to-expansion ratio was about 3.6:1, and base-model AIME2024 pass@8192 was 93.3% versus 83.3% for ProRL-1.5B [7]. This is a direct tension between low-budget accuracy and high-budget coverage. Exploration mechanisms and hybrid objectives should therefore be evaluated on the whole solution distribution, not only its most likely answer.

Finally, reward hacking is not hypothetical. In controlled studies, RLVR-trained models can enumerate labels or exploit extensional verifiers instead of learning an invariant rule; isomorphic perturbation testing exposes this shortcut, and controlled Olmo-3 experiments found weak extensional verification induced a hacking gap while isomorphic verification prevented it [9]. On Activity Scheduling and LIS, RLVR improved answer-level generalization without always improving reasoning; on LIS, answer-only reward caused intermediate reasoning and response length to collapse after a few PPO updates [8]. These findings favor adversarial, isomorphic, process-aware tests and independent verifiers over another round of leaderboard optimization.

## Trends and open problems

The field is moving from “can RL improve a benchmark?” to “what behavior did the reward select?” The immediate trend is modular training: cold-start demonstrations, self-training or rejection sampling, group-relative or PPO-style optimization, and domain-specific verifiers are combined rather than treated as rivals [4][12][5]. The next frontier is broader grounding in code, science, and interactive tools, where rewards can be richer but less perfectly specified [14][15].

Several questions remain unresolved. First, when does answer-only RL reliably improve the reasoning process rather than merely correlate with it? CoT-Pass@K, step-level audits, and isomorphic perturbations are partial answers, but robust process evaluation is still expensive [8][9][2]. Second, how can training preserve diversity and exploration while improving pass@1? Support shrinkage and negative interference suggest that KL regularization alone may not suffice [6][7]. Third, how should systems handle uncertainty? Calibration, abstention, and attempt budgets need to become first-class metrics, especially when longer test-time thinking changes apparent capability [10][6]. Finally, reward datasets and synthetic demonstrations need contamination and exploit screening: even weakly seeded cheating behavior can become an optimized policy under RL. Progress will depend less on a single superior optimizer than on verifiers, data curation, and evaluations that distinguish valid, transferable reasoning from successful reward pursuit.
